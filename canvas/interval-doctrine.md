# Interval Doctrine

*A manifesto for an art of the space between.*

**Interval Doctrine** holds that meaning never lives inside a mark. It lives in the gap between two marks. A single shape is inert; two shapes set at a precise distance make a sentence. The work of this movement is therefore the calibration of gaps: the exact width of a gutter, the measured silence between one aperture and the next, the moment a steady rhythm slips out of step. Each piece is built as an act of patient attention, meticulously crafted until the intervals themselves seem to hold their breath.

**Space and form.** The basic unit is the aperture: a small, softly cornered rectangle, repeated without variation of shape. Hundreds of these openings make a field, and the field is held inside generous, silent margins that the eye feels as pressure. Form is never decorative. It is a vessel for repetition, so that the smallest departure from it, a half-step shift or a single missing beat, lands with the force of an event. Composition follows a strict Swiss grid, but its weight sits off centre, and every alignment is the result of painstaking adjustment, held until nothing could be moved by a hair.

**Color and material.** The palette is restrained to the point of austerity: a warm bone ground like aged archival paper, carbon-black ink laid down in stepped densities, and one accent, a muted safelight red, allowed to appear exactly once. Color does not decorate here. It marks. Ink density changes in tiny, even steps, so the whole surface reads as a slow tide of light and dark, a gradient told through accumulation, not blending. The paper keeps a faint, honest grain: the trace of a physical object that someone studied by hand.

**Scale and rhythm.** Dense repetition at the micro scale meets vast stillness at the macro scale. The viewer first sees a quiet grey field, then rows, then individual cells, then the one place where the order breaks. This staged discovery is the core experience. Rhythm is metrical, counted and regular, so that its single interruption is felt in the body before the mind finds it. Achieving this demands master-level execution: an interval that is off by a fraction loses the illusion of inevitability.

**Text and hierarchy.** Words are rare instruments, never explanations. Typography is thin, small and clinical: index numerals, registration ticks, a plate number, perhaps one line in a soft italic like a whispered marginal note. Text sits in the architecture as a scientist's annotation sits beside a specimen, quiet and exact, never overlapping, always given room to breathe. Hierarchy is carried by scale and spacing, not by weight or color. The finished artifact should look like the product of deep expertise and countless hours: a plate from an imaginary treatise proving that what passes *between* things can be measured.
